Here is why it is worth your five minutes a day.

## The uncomfortable part

My position, and I am not going to phrase it politely:

> 90% of your employees you can simply swap for tokens.

The arithmetic I run in my own head:

| | Cost per month |
|---|---|
| Employee in the Valley | $10,000-15,000 |
| Contractor in the Philippines or India | ~$1,000 |
| Claude or Codex | $200-500 |

Cheaper than the Philippines, and it scales without hiring, onboarding or HR. What is left is a small number of high-level human orchestrators, plus synthetic employees.

**These figures are my estimate, not a measurement.** They come from running this lab, not from a survey. We have a research pass running on the real economics right now; when it comes back, I will replace these numbers with sourced ones and say so. I would rather label an estimate as an estimate than quote a confident number I cannot defend.

## The part nobody tells you

Becoming an orchestrator does not require you to code.

It requires you to do the thing you already do every day: describe what you want to someone who is competent, fast, and has no context about your business. That is a management skill, not an engineering one. Most executives are better at it than most engineers.

The barrier is not intelligence. It is the first fifteen minutes: a black window, a blinking cursor, and the well-founded suspicion that you are about to break something.

That is what this series is for.

## What we actually run here

I do not teach this from theory. This is the lab as it exists tonight:

- Five machines. Each one runs a Claude agent. They message each other through a Telegram group, without me in the middle.
- A second brain: everything I have ever recorded, called, written or researched, in one searchable store.
- A CRM that knows whose turn it is to reply to whom, and raises an alarm when we have been silent for a week.
- Watchdogs that check on other robots, and wake a human for exactly two things: money, and anything irreversible.
- Yesterday that fleet ran 165 robot sessions alongside 67 live ones.

None of it was built by a development team. It was built by one non-programmer describing what he wanted, in words, day after day.

Each lesson in this series takes one piece of that and shows you the working example: what we built, what broke, what it cost. Including the failures. Especially the failures.
